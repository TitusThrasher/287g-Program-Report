Despite the countless other reasons Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) is in the news these days, attention on its 287(g) program has grown significantly since the November 2024 presidential campaign and election.

The trend shows up in news headlines and search data across the country.

That’s to be expected when:

  1. These agreements represent one of the most intimate connections between federal immigration enforcement and local policing/governance.

  2. The program has grown 8 times the size it was exactly one year ago, ballooning from 144 active agreements on January 20, 2025 to 1,317 today.

There are also 41 applications waiting for final rubber stamp approval from Charles Wall, who replaced Madison Sheahan as ICE Deputy Director last week ⤵️

And the program’s growth will almost definitely continue in 2026, for two key reasons:

  • state legislation has already been introduced in Kentucky, South Carolina, and Tennessee in 2026 to effectively require law enforcement agencies with a jail in those states to participate in the program.

    • these bills resemble already-passed legislation in Florida, where all 67 county sheriff’s offices have an active agreement, and Texas, where new legislation took effect January 1st that will push agencies to comply by December 2026.

  • the payroll reimbursement and bounty-style incentives program that DHS started running in October to encourage more law enforcement agencies to join.

Ongoing Stories

First, let’s check in on a few ongoing stories covered here previously:

287(g) in Minnesota

The people of Minnesota deserve our collective attention and support.

Along with recommendations for media members to follow and orgs to support in Minnesota, my ‘Immigration in Minnesota’ guide is meant to help visualize the federal immigration agencies and ICE partnerships in Minnesota ⤵️

This includes a breakdown of 287(g) partners and intergovernmental contracts for jailing infrastructure in the state. It also includes an update on Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison’s opinion from December, on county-level 287(g) participation in the state.

To recap: Ellison’s opinion effectively removes county sheriffs’ ability to unilaterally enter into a 287(g) agreement, instead placing that authority on each county’s board of commissioners.

After Ellison’s opinion was published, the state’s ACLU chapter announced it is suing to end Freeborn County’s 287(g) agreement. The ultimate impact of this opinion on the eight Minnesota sheriffs’ offices with existing 287(g) agreements is unclear.

Kenosha County, WI

On New Year’s Day, I posted my 287(g) ‘Year in Review’, with a breakdown of the program’s growing hold on local policing across the country.

In that post, I mentioned Kenosha County’s Sheriff David Zoerner had released a statement in September 2025 to confirm there were not plans to join the program — because it was not in the “best interest” of the county’s taxpayers.

Which is why I was surprised to find Kenosha County on a list of pending agreements published by ICE in late December, causing me to add this note before publishing:

NOTE/UPDATE:

While reviewing the current list of pending applications published by ICE, I noticed that Kenosha County has a “Jail Enforcement Model” application pending. Whether this is an admin error, or a notable update in Kenosha County’s participation, will be clear(er) once the next list of approved agreements is published.

From a brief search, I haven’t found any public indications the sheriff’s stance has changed, so this pending application is worth following.

Unfortunately, it did end up being a notable update: Kenosha’s agreement for the program’s Jail Enforcement Model was signed by Zoerner on December 18, 2025, and countersigned by Madison Sheahan as ICE Deputy Director on January 7th, 2026.

The state’s ACLU chapter has already condemned Zoerner’s decision, and local Wisconsin media from the Wisconsin Examiner to the Kenosha News have covered the new development.

From a January 14th press release from ACLU of Wisconsin:

“While the sheriff asserts he needs the 287(g) agreement to do his job, he is wrong. His department is fully capable of keeping people in Kenosha County safe without getting involved in federal immigration operations, and his participation in 287(g) does not grant him authority to honor administrative immigration detainers, which we believe is illegal under state law.

The sheriff’s refusal to be adequately transparent about signing on to a 287(g) agreement is unacceptable. We call on Sheriff Zoerner to withdraw from the program immediately and stop making decisions that impact public safety in Kenosha County behind closed doors.”

And for more of my own thoughts re: Sheriff Zoerner’s new statement, which he published nearly a month after signing the agreement — and only after it received media attention ⤵️

The Cash Cow

So what changed in Kenosha County in the 3 months between Sheriff Zoerner’s statement in September and his signing a 287(g) application in mid-December? Easy: the “best interest” of county taxpayers, as Zoerner phrased it.

In October, ICE started sending checks to participating agencies as part of a new payroll reimbursement and bounty-style incentives program. It’s part of the roughly $14 billion in new OBBBA funding for immigration enforcement specifically dedicated to buying growing ICE’s partnership network with local and state agencies.

For more on the new 287(g) cash cow:

We can already see how this money is working its way through the law enforcement complex via local news coverage and policy analysis in several states:

Florida: Suncoast News in the Tampa Bay, Florida area reported Monday that 5 local agencies already received a combined $1.78 million in ICE reimbursements. The Port Richey Police Chief Frank Ruggiero confirmed his agency’s funds will go to license plate reader technology.

Kentucky: Analysis published last week by the Kentucky Center for Economic Policy covers the growing network of 287(g) agencies in the state, including a helpful comparison based on the OBBBA’s signing in July 2025:

“Twelve of the agreements happened after the OBBBA became law, all but two of which were for the Task Force Model. Since we first published this analysis in October of 2025, eight new 287(g) agreements have been entered into, all of which are the Task Force Model, and an additional Task Force Model contract is pending for the McCracken County Sheriff’s Office.”

Tennessee: A report from Tennessee Lookout from November does a great job of framing the growth in participation in the state back to the new financial incentives.

Texas: This article from ABC’s East Texas affiliate last week explains how agencies in the state can double up on cash incentives.

In addition to ICE’s new reimbursement and quota program, Texas counties with less than one million residents are now eligible for a new state-level grant program, with grants ranging from $80,000 to $140,000.1

“The Comptroller’s office has distributed application links directly to every county sheriff’s office in Texas. Applications opened on Jan. 1, and fund disbursement will begin in March, with additional funds to be distributed on a rolling basis as additional qualified counties apply for the program.”

Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts (January 16, 2026)

Pennsylvania: Notably, the new recruitment pitch seems to have backfired with at least one participating agency.

Montour County Sheriff William McKenna recently explained that while his office technically has an active 287(g) agreement with ICE signed in June 2025, the addition of financial incentives led him to hold off on sending officers for program training:2

“‘This is what led me to not send anyone through the training, as that would conflict with Pennsylvania law, which prohibits quotas,’ the sheriff said.

‘Our office is not refusing to work with any agency, or not saying we will not help any agency if they ask us. We just are not participating in the program because of the last incentive. I would not want to risk any of my staff getting in trouble over something like that.’”

That seems particularly relevant for the 51 other Pennsylvania law enforcement agencies currently participating. This includes 12 county-level agencies, after new Bucks County Sheriff Danny Ceisler ended his office’s agreement shortly after taking office earlier this month (more on that below).

As I noted in recent analysis of the nearly 400 U.S. cities and towns that have joined the program in the past year, Pennsylvania’s municipal-level participation deserves attention:

Pennsylvania has the 2nd most towns/cities participating. Far behind Florida’s whopping 186 municipal agreements, but PA has 38 active municipalities covering a population of at least 200,000 — notably, most of these are for borough and township constable’s offices."

State Mandates

With Florida and Texas as precedent, we’ve already seen legislators in three states introduce bills this month that would effectively require law enforcement agencies with a jail or correctional facility to participate.

In Tennessee, the 287(g) mandate was introduced as part of a far-reaching package of immigration enforcement bills from TN Republicans, who hope to align state-level policy with Trump’s Department of Homeland Security. A January 15th article from Nashville’s News Channel 5 notes that Stephen Miller personally worked with state legislators to draft the legislation.

It’s also important to note that Tennessee was recently revealed as a top candidate to host a new “National Coordination Center” for DHS/ICE, responsible for dispatching armed ICE contractors to 287(g) program jails to speed up their new deportation army.

I wrote more about this, and other recent ICE contractor requests —including a plan to build concentration camps out of Amazon warehouses — in late December ⤵️

In South Carolina, the proposed bill includes administrative penalties for noncompliant sheriffs’ offices, including a pause in state grants from the SC Department of Public Safety, as well as extra compliance reporting and oversight requirements with the state.

And in Kentucky, Republicans introduced two 287(g) bills last week:

  • the more extensive proposal would require every local and state law enforcement agency to participate in all three program models;

  • a second, relatively constrained bill would require the Kentucky State Police to have an active Task Force Model agreement.

Interestingly, the constraints in the second bill seem to be the result of pressure from local law enforcement against a blanket mandate. From the Courier Journal (January 14th):

“Roberts said he initially drafted the bill to require all local agencies to adopt the Task Force model, similar to Senate Bill 86. But he said he narrowed the language to apply only to KSP after local agencies told him they were interested in partnering with ICE voluntarily.”

I’ll also highlight that it’s not all “roses and weekly ICE checks” for local governments in Texas and Florida.

In El Paso, the Border Network for Human Rights organized a town hall this weekend with the sheriff’s office to discuss implementation of Texas Senate Bill 8 in El Paso County.

“[Sheriff Ugarte] said the warrant service approach is the least intrusive and best suited for El Paso County.

‘There is a lot of confusion and a lot of fear because of what is happening across the nation,’ Ugarte said. ‘That is why it is important for us to be transparent and to talk directly with the community.’

Residents raised concerns about recent federal enforcement activity and the impact on families, including fears about raids and the role of local law enforcement.” – KVIA (January 18, 2026)

And hundreds of protestors organized in Kyle, Texas this weekend in response to ICE enforcement activity in the area, demanding Hays County Sheriff Anthony Hipolito refrain from joining the program.

An article published by The Texas Tribune this week discusses how the program’s growth is impacting ICE arrests in the state:

“A new state law passed in last year’s legislative session requires sheriffs in all counties with a jail to enter into such agreements, also known as the 287(g) program, this year. Since Trump returned to the White House, more than half of ICE arrests in Texas have come from local jails, according to the Tribune analysis.” (emphasis added)

And while Governor DeSantis cheers on the 10,000 arrests3 supposedly facilitated through Florida’s push for statewide 287(g) participation, we’re already seeing the devastating impact on K-12 school attendance. As a local NPR article last week notes:

“Florida’s Office of Economic and Demographic Research reported that the number of students expected to enroll in the 2025-2026 school year in public and private settings dropped by 46,455 students from their last estimation in April.

(…) While enrollment overall is still growing, the report states that the lower estimate ‘signals that the universe of K-12 enrollment is atypically contracting’ and that the most likely reason is ‘related to the chilling effects from recently implemented immigration policies.’” (emphasis added)

Florida is also unique for its 15 college and university police departments that participate. Last week, I shared an article on an ongoing protest at the University of North Florida in Jacksonville:

And similar efforts have emerged at other schools, including the University of Florida, Florida International, and the University of South Florida.

New Stories

Here are some new(er) stories that I’m watching as well:

Bucks County, PA

You may have seen some coverage on the new Bucks County Sheriff, Danny Ceisler, who took office earlier this month and quickly canceled the county’s Task Force Model 287(g) agreement.

Ceisler deserves recognition for swiftly ending the agreement, which was a key piece to his campaign platform last year. But his expanded stance should also be noted, as it doesn’t really stray too far from the county’s previous cooperation with ICE:

“Ceisler said his department will continue to share information with ICE, which will still have access to the county jail. But the sheriff’s office will not target people living and working in the community.

“We are continuing to maintain an open dialogue with ICE and all our federal and state law enforcement partners. By any definition of a sanctuary county, we are not one,” Ceisler told CBS News Philadelphia.”

New Hampshire FOIA

Last week, the ACLU of New Hampshire filed a lawsuit after ICE failed to respond to a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request for documentation used in training NH law enforcement as part of the 287(g) program.

“The lawsuit argues that the public has a right to know how 13 New Hampshire law enforcement agencies—including New Hampshire’s largest police force, the State Police—will enforce a federal immigration program that was previously suspended in 2012 following racial profiling concerns. Especially after the recent killing of Renee Nicole Good in Minneapolis, it is critical to learn how law enforcement officers who are engaging in immigration enforcement are trained.”

It will be interesting to see what comes out of this lawsuit, and whether it results in any new evidence on how ICE is training and coordinating with partner agencies.

Also in New Hampshire: state Democrats just introduced a new bill to restrict local and county governments from using taxpayer funds or government facilities to assist with immigration enforcement/detention. However, the bill explicitly (and conveniently) carves out an exception for 287(g) participants.

There are currently 13 agencies in the state with active agreements:

  • the NH State Police Department

  • four of the state’s ten counties (including its two largest, Rockingham and Hillsborough)

  • eight local police departments

Virginia Governor Spanberger

Another high-profile move to act on a 287(g)-related electoral campaign promise came from new Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger.

Shortly after her inauguration on Saturday, Spanberger repealed former Governor Youngkin’s Executive Order 47, which required Virginia’s State Police and Department of Corrections to participate in the program. As noted by the Virginia Scope, both agencies still need to actually terminate their 287(g) agreements, which is a separate move from Spanberger’s directive.

Similarly, Virginia’s NPR affiliate added a correction to an article from Saturday, to note the new governor’s office had clarified that the new EO gives Spanberger “the option” to end the two state-level 287(g) agreements.4

Meanwhile, the state’s ACLU chapter quickly put out a statement Sunday that recognized Spanberger’s order as a “first step”:

“And this executive order is just the start: to make the governor’s vision of a Commonwealth where all Virginians are safe into a reality, the governor must terminate the state’s existing 287(g) agreements, and Virginia lawmakers must deliver meaningful legislation to her desk to protect Virginians’ private data and sensitive places like courthouses and schools from immigration enforcement. Together, Virginia’s leaders have the power to get state and local police out of federal civil immigration enforcement once and for all.”

Mississippi Grant Program

A new bill was introduced in the Mississippi Senate this week to create a grant program to encourage more local and county agencies to join the 287(g) program. Unlike the statewide mandate proposals in Kentucky, South Carolina, and Tennessee, this bill appears to be strictly an incentives-based program — if passed, the grants would distributed by the state’s Department of Public Safety to participating agencies.

Conclusions

The patterns here all boil down to one theme: follow the money. That starts with the $14+ billion in new recruitment money fueling the new 287(g) bounty system, in itself just a small portion of DHS’ new war chest.

This money, in turn, helps explain the renewed focus on 287(g)-related legislation from Republican legislators in multiple states this month. It also helps explain the program’s popularity with sheriffs’ offices — a significant driver of growth considering one in five U.S. counties have an active agreement.

But the news coverage also shows what is possible with organized, informed resistance. This program, like everything else ICE these days, is not popular with the U.S. public, especially once the details are better understood. We’re starting to see that play out across the country.
