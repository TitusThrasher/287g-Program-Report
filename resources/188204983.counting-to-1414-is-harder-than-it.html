The 287(g) program continues its record growth: there are now 1,414 active agreements between ICE and local law enforcement agencies nationwide. 1,280 of these agreements have been signed since January 20, 2025. 264 were signed in the past 3 months. 106 in the past 30 days.

Tracking this data isn’t as easy as it might sound though. Not because it requires any complex math, but because ICE publishes it in a way that makes for a huge headache when trying to identify changes or analyze trends.

That’s a big part of why I started our interactive report on 287(g) agreements — to make this data useful to the public by cleaning up errors, adding geographic and population context, and preserving historical records that would otherwise disappear from this analysis.

Snapshots from ICE (and why it’s a problem)

Every day or so, ICE uploads a new Excel spreadsheet to its website listing all active 287(g) agreements. That’s it. No changelog. No historical archive. No record of what changed since yesterday.

When an agreement is canceled — whether it’s terminated by a city council vote, a governor’s executive order, a decision from an elected sheriff, a court ruling, or Attorney General decision — it simply disappears from the next version of the list. ICE doesn’t publish a “canceled agreements” file. Once canceled, the agreement effectively disappears from the federal public record.

This approach to publishing a standard data set is called a snapshot; basically, ICE publishes a new static list of the agreements that are active each day — everything that’s true at that moment.

A changelog, meanwhile, tracks changes over time: what was added, removed, or updated (and when). The difference matters:

With a changelog, you can look up: “When was the agreement with Pike County terminated?”

With a snapshot, you’re only able to answer that question if you also have a reference copy of the data before the agreement disappeared. If you want to look at trends over time, like growth rates or the impact of state policy changes, you have to build your own historical archive by saving reference snapshots to compare back to.

Government transparency shouldn’t require citizens to run their own analytics and file backup systems.

Human Error + Fuzzy Matching

The snapshot-only approach would be somewhat workable if the data was clean. It’s not.

ICE’s published data contains typos, inconsistent formatting, and no way to uniquely identify each agreement. “Somervell Sheriff’s Office” becomes “Somervell County Sheriff’s Office.” Extra spaces creep in. Abbreviations vary.

This creates a real problem when you’re trying to detect cancellations. For example, let’s say we compare lists from yesterday and today, and notice that “Berrian County” is missing. Was that agreement canceled? Or did someone at ICE just fix the spelling to “Berrien County”?

To figure this out, we use a technique called fuzzy matching: finding text that’s similar but not identical. It’s similar to the way your phone might show you “Jon Smith” when you search for “John Smith”. This approach becomes more difficult as more agreements are added to the program over time, more untracked changes are made by ICE, etc.

The 1-to-Many Dilemma

Our ability to easily track agreements is complicated further by the fact that each participating agency can have up to three active agreements across the different 287(g) program models — each with a different (but sometimes the same) effective date.

When a single sheriff’s office can have multiple agreements active simultaneously, it complicates how you count and report the data. “1,414 active agreements” and “1,064 participating agencies” are both accurate, but tell different stories. Depending on the question, you might need to count total agreements, unique agencies, or unique agency-state combinations.

A simple solution would be for ICE to assign each agreement a unique identifier, or primary key: a single tracking number that stays constant even when typos are corrected or agency names change. That ID almost certainly exists in ICE’s internal systems, but not shared publicly.

Instead, we’re forced to identify agreements using what’s called a composite key: combining state + agency name + agreement type + signature date. Together, these values usually identify a single record. But if any component changes — a typo gets fixed, or a date is corrected — the composite key changes with it. While it’s a useful workaround for the constraints in ICE’s published data, composite keys are fragile and not a sustainable solution at scale.

What We Do

Because of these limitations in ICE’s raw data, we maintain our own cleaned and enriched version. Here’s my process:

When ICE publishes a new list:

  1. Filter by signature date to identify agreements added since the last update

  2. Process those new entries—fixing typos, trimming whitespace, standardizing agency names

  3. Enrich the data with information ICE doesn’t provide, like geographic coordinates for mapping and county population figures from the U.S. Census

  4. Append the cleaned entries to our master dataset

To detect cancellations:

  1. Compare our total count against ICE’s new total

  2. If the numbers don’t match — let’s say our dataset has 1,318 entries but ICE shows 1,315 — we know agreements were removed

  3. Flag anything in our data that’s missing from ICE’s list

  4. Check each missing entry for “close matches” in the new data (suggesting a typo fix or similar edit, instead of a true cancellation)

  5. Verify any unmatched entries against external sources (e.g. local news coverage of city council or county board votes)

That last step is important, by the way. When I flagged Coraopolis Borough, PA as a potential cancellation, it was local news coverage of a borough council vote that confirmed it. The data pointed me in the right direction, and journalism validated the finding.

By the way, you can now view a list of terminated agreements in our report — but this list only includes agreements canceled after December 08, 2025 (when I started explicitly tracking this).

287(g) News + Data

All of this might sound like abstract “data hygiene”, but the past few weeks help demonstrate how this connects to the larger story of the 287(g) program’s massive growth — and the massive rise in public backlash. With the way ICE manages this data (and canceled agreements in particular) there’s no public record of what changed, or why. For context, here are some of the recent ‘whys’:

Legal challenges: In Pinal County, Arizona, the Board of Supervisors declared the county attorney’s 287(g) agreement illegal and void, arguing he had no authority to enter the county into a federal agreement without board approval.

When the county attorney, Brad Miller, chose to ignore the Board’s order to halt further participation, they filed a lawsuit — and were granted a temporary restraining order last week. So, is this agreement active or not? The legal dispute won’t appear in ICE’s spreadsheet.

State reversals: Just days after taking office in January, Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger rescinded former Gov. Youngkin’s executive order directing state agencies to enter 287(g) agreements. Then, in early February, Spanberger ordered the four state police agencies in Virginia with active agreements to each end their participation immediately.

Three of the four agencies have been removed from the published list over the past two weeks — but the agreement with the Virginia Department of Corrections’ Law Enforcement Services agency is still listed. For the canceled agreements, there is now zero evidence they ever existed based on ICE’s public data, let alone when or how they were terminated.

Pending legislation: A bill to ban 287(g) agreements in Maryland is expected to be signed by Governor Wes Moore soon, after it quickly moved through both houses of the state legislature. And New Mexico lawmakers recently moved to amend existing House Bill 9, the ‘Immigrant Safety Act’, in an attempt to ban 287(g) agreements statewide.

Meanwhile, Alabama is moving legislation in the opposite direction, explicitly authorizing local agencies to enter immigration enforcement agreements. This is on the heels of Republican-led efforts in states like South Carolina, Kentucky, and Tennessee — and already-enacted legislation in Texas and Florida — to essentially mandate many of their states’ local law enforcement agencies to participate.

For more on these efforts, see my post “ICE’s Badges for Bounties Program” from last month:

Community resistance: In Butler County, Ohio, residents have packed commissioners’ meetings for 25 consecutive weeks to oppose the sheriff’s ICE contract. In Mount Joy Borough, Pennsylvania, the council put a signed agreement on hold after residents demanded more transparency. Student-led protests have taken hold at several of the 15 colleges and universities in Florida where campus police departments participate.

Tribal sovereignty: The Oglala Sioux Tribal Council unanimously voted against participating in the 287(g) program, and to ban ICE activity on the Pine Ridge Reservation in southwestern South Dakota entirely — an area over twice as large as Rhode Island.

When these developments aren’t accurately represented in ICE’s published data, the legal disputes, community resistance, and policy debates that shaped them are effectively erased from the federal record for this program.

Snapshots of Accountability

Without a changelog, unique identifiers, or even just a “status” column with dates, the public record is whatever ICE says it is. Equipped with a blank check budget and a wide range of AI surveillance tech from Palantir and friends, yet publishing low-quality spreadsheets — it’s an intentional, half-assed effort by ICE to muddy public accountability.

When acting ICE director Todd Lyons compared his goals for immigration enforcement and deportations as ‘Amazon Prime, but for human beings’ last year, he wasn’t joking. In fact, he’s taking a move right from the Bezos playbook by ramping up detention capacity through ICE’s new warehouse-to-concentration camp business model. Unlike Amazon, though, Todd’s agency can’t seem to figure out even the most basic data management tasks.

This is why we maintain our own clean, enriched, time-series data on 287(g) agreements. To help preserve evidence of how this program has evolved, recognizing that each update represents a real decision by a real official, board, or community vote on how local law enforcement resources will be used.

These agreements may be federal, but the decisions behind them are local. Those local decisions deserve to be documented, not erased.
