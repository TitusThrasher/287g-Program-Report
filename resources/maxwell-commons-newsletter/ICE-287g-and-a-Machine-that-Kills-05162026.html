For Orlín, Liam, the students at Funston Elementary, and the countless other children separated from family, jailed, or otherwise harmed by our federal government’s immigration enforcement + deportation machine.

Yesterday, U.S. Representatives Delia Ramirez (IL) and Sylvia Garcia (TX), joined by a number of Democratic House colleagues, hosted a shadow hearing on the 287(g) program.

And thanks to all of you readers + supporters, this little newsletter made an appearance! I’m extremely grateful Congresswoman Ramirez and her team gave me the opportunity to contribute a small piece to this important event.

“What’s a shadow hearing?”, you might ask. Basically, it’s an unofficial congressional hearing organized by the minority party.

They aren’t necessarily a feature of Congress historically1, but they’ve become more common in the current Congress, largely in response to Republicans refusal to conduct any actual oversight of the Trump administration’s actions. The absence of accountability across the Department of Homeland Security and its component agencies, including ICE and CBP, is particularly dangerous. That’s why this particular hearing was so important.

The Immediate Harm

The hearing started with powerful testimony from five witnesses who spoke on the impact of these partnerships and the harm created by ICE’s growing influence in local policing across the country.

This included a statement from Shayln Fluharty, the attorney representing a mother, Wendy Hernandez Reyes, who was recently deported to Honduras after a 287(g) traffic stop in Florida. The tragic result of Wendy’s cruel abduction — the death of her 3-year old son Orlín — was the focus of a new Washington Post article from Maria Sacchetti, published this morning.

The Harm That’s Incentivized

We also heard testimony on how these agreements actively incentivize racial profiling in the local police departments and sheriffs’ offices that participate. Earlier this week, The Guardian’s Stephen Starr published a story about a bizarre and incredibly concerning situation involving two police officers from Gratis, Ohio.

Share

As local cops for the small, all-white town in rural Ohio, they drove roughly 50 miles outside their jurisdiction to an area of Cincinnati with a significant Latine, Spanish-speaking population. The officers tried to enter several schools in the neighborhood, claiming they were conducting '“wellness checks” on behalf of ICE. School administrators who turned them away said the officers had a list of up to 30 students’ names.2

How does this even happen? As I note in this video recap of the story, it’s the inevitable outcome of a program where:

  • perverse incentives are baked in;

  • the scope of authority and responsibility is intentionally ambiguous; and

  • the public has little insight into program operations.

But what even are these "wellness checks", and how do they relate to ICE and its 287(g) partner agencies?

The short answer: no one seems to really know. But it seems ICE is now encouraging its partner agencies to take on this work as well, buried in a service-level contract that’s not public record. When an agency joins the program, the only document we get from ICE is a boilerplate ‘Memorandum of Agreement’ signed by each agency, with little vetting before a senior official at ICE counter-signs.

How do I know that? Back in March, and I covered the first Task Force Model 287(g) agreement signed by a school district police force.

After I first noted the agreement in this newsletter, the agreement with the Caney Valley Public Schools police department was quietly removed just two days later. We were told it was a misunderstanding, after the school resource officer (and head of the two-officer department) was pushed to sign up for the 287(g) program by a mysterious representative at a sexual assault investigation training.

According to the responsible officer and the school district superintendent, the error stemmed from an interest in training opportunities to meet their annual officer training requirements.3 No word on whether it’s common for law enforcement trainings to require you to e-sign a 14-page contract and email it back just to “learn more information” about a course.

And just like that, every adult involved in this “mistake” moved on, with no appetite for further investigation.

The Harm Done Over Time

As neighbors continue to lose trust in their local government for things like emergency first response services, it’s reasonable to assume a chilling effect as a result. That chill can impact everything from access to healthcare services and public transportation, to a decline in job opportunities and lasting damage to local economies.

There’s also the ongoing structural harm to our way of government when a back-alley slush fund — run by a violent, lawless paramilitary force — can be used to unilaterally reshape the annual budgets for hundreds, if not thousands, of law enforcement agencies across the country.

As Rep. Joaquin Castro (TX) highlighted in yesterday’s hearing, small police departments and sheriffs’ offices have already turned to the new reimbursement incentives tied to 287(g) participation in order to balance their budgets. It doesn’t require much imagination to see a not-so-distant future where ICE is an entrenched source of cash, surveillance technology, vehicles, and even targets for local enforcement activity. In fact, it’s already here.

The deportation machine meets the policing industrial complex. Unfortunately, with very deadly results.
