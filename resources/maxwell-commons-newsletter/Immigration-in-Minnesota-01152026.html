Minnesota’s Immigrants

First, some big picture numbers on immigration + Minnesota, from the American Immigration Council’s Map the Impact (based on 2023 American Community Survey data):

There are about 482,400 immigrants living in Minnesota, which is about 8.4% of the state’s population. That’s significantly lower than the national percentage (14.3%) of immigrants among the total U.S. population.

82.9% of Minnesota’s foreign-born population are ‘working age’ (16 to 64), compared to 60.5% of the U.S.-born population.

“In the United States, immigrants are more likely to be working-age than their U.S.-born counterparts. This means they are more likely to be active in the labor force, allowing them to contribute to the economy not only as consumers but also as taxpayers, helping fund social services and programs like Medicare and Social Security.”

Only 10.6% of Minnesota’s foreign-born population are 65+ years old, whereas 18.5% of U.S-born residents have aged out of the ‘working age’ population.

That’s important when considering the broader impact of immigrants on the state’s economy, from tax contributions to entrepreneurial growth:

Approximately 339,100 Minnesota immigrants work, representing about 10.8% of the state’s labor force, and pay more than $6.2 billion in taxes annually — including roughly $2.3 billion to Social Security + $583 million to Medicare + $2.3 billion in state and local taxes. And the combined income of Minnesota’s immigrant households is about $23.1 billion, representing a total spending power of roughly $17 billion.

Just as we see across the country, immigrants in Minnesota are also business starters. An estimated 28,000 immigrant entrepreneurs live in the state, representing over $667 million in annual business income. There are also six (6) Fortune 500 companies founded by immigrants or children of immigrants in the state.

Roughly 13.5% of the state’s immigrants were born in Mexico, followed by India (7.2%), Somalia (7%), Ethiopia (4.7%), and Laos (4.2%). A further sign of Minnesota’s melting pot dynamic: this means Minnesota’s five largest immigrant communities are from countries on three different continents (Africa, Asia, and North America).

And with ample evidence ICE and CBP have illegally abducted dozens of refugees in the MSP area over the past weeks: there are roughly 96,800 individuals living in Minnesota who entered the country under the refugee program. This includes years of security vetting and waiting abroad before entering the U.S.

Over 99% of these Minnesotans are employed, and 85.4% are naturalized citizens — individuals who’ve done everything asked of them, over the course of many years, to become citizens of this country under the U.S. Constitution.1

Department of Justice

Before we get to the Department of Homeland Security, let’s talk about the immigration courts.

The Fort Snelling Immigration Court is the only permanent presence in the state from the Department of Justice’s Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR). Like every other EOIR court in the country, Fort Snelling has an immense backlog of immigration cases, with more than 37,000 cases pending.

While EOIR is a DOJ agency, the court offices are located inside the Whipple Federal Building, which is also home to ICE and other DHS law enforcement offices — muddying trust in an already not-so-independent court system.

With the ongoing escalation of Operation Metro Surge, the Whipple Building and other facilities on the Fort Snelling campus are now being used as a main operating base for ICE, CBP, FBI, and other federal agents in the state.

The building is also being used by ICE to detain individuals swept up by their roving caravans, agitated neighbors who’ve shown up to document their terror, and potentially several unhoused members of the Oglala Sioux Tribe. That’s right, ICE may be holding multiple Native Americans, in the remnants of an old Army fort used as a source of violence against their people generations ago.

Department of Homeland Security (DHS)

All three of the primary immigration agencies under the DHS umbrella have offices in Minneapolis and/or St. Paul:

U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS)

The DHS agency responsible for administering the ‘legal immigration’ system, USCIS, has a St. Paul/Minneapolis (SPM) Field Office in downtown Minneapolis.2 However, the agency’s Chicago Asylum Office has jurisdiction over USCIS-based asylum cases in Minnesota; though the agency does maintain a satellite asylum office for interviews in St. Paul.

USCIS also has two Application Support Centers in Minnesota, in St. Paul and Duluth. USCIS operates dozens of ASCs (think of a DMV) across the U.S., as well as certain high-travel cities abroad, to assist with application-related biometric services. As part of the USCIS application process, individuals may be scheduled for a biometric appointment at the nearest ASC in order to provide fingerprints, updated photographs, and/or an electronic signature.

Customs and Border Protection (CBP)

Responsible for border security and processing travelers (and customs) at ports of entry, including airports, land crossings, and seaports. There are two major CBP components:

U.S. Border Patrol, which includes Greg Bovino + the heavily-armed SWAT-style BORTAC and BORSTAR teams deployed in Minneapolis, Chicago, LA, and elsewhere. Aside from the current occupation, Border Patrol employees in Minnesota typically fall under the Grand Forks, ND sector of the U.S.-Canadian border, and maintain small offices near several of the state’s ports of entry (otherwise staffed by CBP field officers ⤵️.

CBP’s Office of Field Operations, meanwhile, are the officials you interact with when entering the U.S. at an airport or land crossing.

A third element, Air and Marine Operations, assists Border Patrol with air and maritime border security (e.g. drone surveillance and helicopter support).

Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE)

Both of ICE’s primary law enforcement components, Enforcement and Removal Operations (ERO) and Homeland Security Investigations (HSI), have field offices at Fort Snelling. Unfortunately, we have very little insight as to how many people are actually being detained inside this complex right now.

Fort Snelling is also home to agency fields offices for the Office of the Principal Legal Advisor (OPLA), which staffs the 1,200+ ICE attorneys who represent the federal government in EOIR immigration court cases.

ICE’s Local Partners in Minnesota

Along with the presence of federal agencies in the state, the Department of Homeland Security also maintains partnerships with local law enforcement agencies through its 287(g) program, as well as intergovernmental service agreements (IGSAs) that let them outsource ICE detention to beds in local jails.

There are currently 8 Minnesota counties with active 287(g) agreements, each signed in 2025 — covering a population of more than 360,000.

Three of those counties — Sherburne, Kandiyohi, and Freeborn — also have active IGSAs with ICE. These agreements allow ICE to ‘buy’ bed space from county jails and state prison systems, expanding their detention capacity and clouding the separation between civil immigration detention and correctional facilities housing sentenced criminals.

For more info on the relationship between these local jails and ICE detention, check out this briefing from the Prison Policy Initiative (December 2025), an update to their ‘Hiding in Plain Sight’ report from July 2025 using data from the Deportation Data Project.

I also recommend the new ICE detention dashboard from and the Relevant Research team, which makes it really easy to search and locate key details for each ICE detention facility.

And finally, the Vera Institute of Justice’s ICE Detention Trends interactive map, published in December:

Based on current agreements, these county jails can take on 200+ ICE detainees at any given time, which does not include any ICE detainees held (or arrested) under the counties’ 287(g) participation. Importantly, each is within 100 miles of the Fort Snelling base of operations where ICE/CBP/FBI are now staging thousands of federal agents under ‘Operation Metro Surge’.

These IGSA contracts are particularly important for a state like Minnesota, where private prisons were effectively banned through state legislation passed in 2023 and signed by Governor Walz.

Despite this ban, the federal government is reportedly looking to reopen a private CoreCivic facility in Appleton, MN that could theoretically hold 1,600 people. For reference, CoreCivic and its competitor, GEO Group, are two of ICE’s largest vendors for their private prison facilities (and contract guards), alternative-to-detention devices (like ankle monitors), and transportation services.

Legal Battles in the State

Two weeks ago, I noted in my year-end summary of the 287(g) program that Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison had issued a formal opinion on participation in the state in mid-December:

“In Minnesota, the state’s Attorney General just issued a formal opinion on December 12 clarifying that state law does not permit sheriffs to unilaterally enter into 287(g) agreements with ICE. Instead, this authority rests with each county’s board of commissioners. The AG opinion also found that 287(g) agreements don’t give law enforcement the power to detain people solely on civil ICE detainers if state law doesn’t otherwise permit the person’s detention.”

After Ellison’s opinion was published, the state’s ACLU chapter announced it is suing to end Freeborn County’s 287(g) agreement. The ultimate impact of this opinion on the eight Minnesota sheriffs’ offices with existing 287(g) agreements is unclear.

In the wake of Renee Good’s murder last week, AG Ellison held a press conference with Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty to highlight concerning restrictions on state-level participation in the shooting investigation, and to ask the MN public to submit evidence.

On Monday, Ellison officially filed a lawsuit against DHS (including ICE + CBP) leadership on behalf of Minnesotans, with the cities of St. Paul and Minneapolis joining as plaintiffs. For more on that lawsuit, here’s helpful analysis from MPR News.
