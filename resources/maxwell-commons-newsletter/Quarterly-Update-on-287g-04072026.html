Big Picture Numbers

According to ICE’s newest list, published yesterday (April 6th), the agency has 1,630 active 287(g) agreements with 1,379 local and state law enforcement agencies. Of the active agreements:

  • 1,499 were signed after January 20, 2025.

  • 379 were signed in the past 90 days (nearly 50% more than the previous 3-month stretch).

  • 135 were signed in the past 30 days (nearly the same as were added in the previous 30 days)

These partnerships span 39 states and two U.S. territories (Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands), including 350 agreements in Texas and 344 in Florida. Participating agencies include:

75 state-level agreements

75 state-level agreements in 24 different states + Guam and CNMI, including the fish and wildlife conservation offices in Florida and Louisiana, and the Massachusetts Departments of Corrections (and 19 other state prison systems). From Jason Koebler at 404 Media yesterday, on what the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission’s 287(g) partnership looks like:

Police with the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission are able to do these lookups for ICE because in August, Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis enrolled nearly 800 of its officers in 287(g), a Department of Homeland Security (DHS) program that gives state and local police certain immigration enforcement powers.

543 municipal-level agreements

543 local police department agreements, covering municipalities as small as Jamestown, SC (pop. 73) and Indian Creek, FL (pop. 89) to large cities including Las Vegas, NV (pop. 678,922); Mesa, AZ (pop. 517,151); and Miami, FL (pop. 487,014). The 190 municipal agreements in Florida cover a total population of nearly 7 million residents, alongside 17 university and college campus PDs in the state that have signed on.

1,012 county-Level agreements

1,012 county-level agreements across 739 unique counties, or 23.5% of all county-level jurisdictions in the U.S. Participating county agencies (primarily sheriffs’ offices) cover an average of over 127,000 residents — significantly more than the average population across all 3,144 U.S. counties, at 108,177 (all according to the U.S. Census 2024 ACS).

Why Is The Program Growing?

There isn’t one single factor behind the massive increase in sign-ups since Trump took office last January. Instead, you can roughly separate the list of factors into a number of carrots and sticks:

The carrots

Some factors are incentive-based, like the payroll and equipment/vehicle reimbursements that the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) enthusiastically added to the program last fall, in what I had referred to as the new 287(g) cash cow.

For example, Wired recently published records showing a $122,515 payment to the police department in Carroll, New Hampshire — a town with about 820 residents where the ICE payments will now essentially cover the department’s payroll.

How do the payments work?

As Wired’s Sammy Sussman explains in this great interview with NHPR, the sign-up process itself has contributed to our limited understanding on how these agreements actually work.

Similar to my own findings, and those of a number of journalists I’ve spoken to who are following this storyline, Sussman notes the huge gap in transparent records re: payments that have actually been distributed from DHS to these agencies:

“Carroll, I believe, is one of the first departments, at least that has provided records of payments to reporters, to be clear. I filed a number of records requests, as reporters do, around the country, and I didn’t find many other records of significant payments in the way that I saw them in Carroll. (…) but I believe that they’re one of the first departments to actually see that money come through.”

And we’re learning more specifics on how ICE lawyers are using service-level contract agreements to help limit transparency:

Basically, they’ve added in various provisions that govern the release of information, but they’ve added that in a service agreement that’s purportedly about the exchanging of funds or essentially the federal government paying Carroll. So it’s this new way to introduce a separate set of contractual obligations in this separate document that we didn’t already know about.

Each additional neighboring county that signed a 287(g) agreement increased a county’s own adoption likelihood by 37.5%, the single strongest predictor in the model, larger than partisanship, population size, fiscal conditions, or demographics.

30 minutes or less

To manage the proliferation, we’ve seen state legislators in several states propose or successfully establish “immigration enforcement cooperation” agencies, like Tennessee’s new Centralized Immigration Enforcement Division. And middleman grant programs managed by state offices, like Florida’s State Board of Immigration Enforcement and the Acting Texas Comptroller’s Sheriff Immigration Law Enforcement Grant Program, announced in January.

As I wrote in late December, ICE has floated a variety of operational initiatives to improve cooperation with participating agencies, including a National Coordination Center to be based in Tennessee.

In Texas, DHS posted a request for information (RFI) as a first step to hiring armed transport contractors to speed up the custody transfer of detainees to/from participating jails. Like a pizza delivery ad, the RFI mentions the ideal contractor will be able to meet a 30-minute arrival time to participating jails and other detention facilities.

Some of the carrots aren’t exactly new. Before the reimbursement incentives added last fall, DHS/ICE covered a much smaller portion of participating agencies’ expenses for training and limited program-related tech expenses. While that arrangement didn’t have the same financial appeal as today’s incentives, some smaller departments have even signed up simply because of the program’s training credits for participating officers.

This includes a very brief appearance last month from the Caney Valley Public Schools police department, which marked the first K-12 school district police force to sign up for the program.

It was quickly canceled 48-hours later, after inquiries from local journalists who had seen my post from the day the agreement was added to ICE’s list last month. In the weeks since, we’ve learned quite a bit about ICE’s recruitment strategy and the apparent lack of internal vetting of new agreements.

The Sticks

It’s important to note why states like Texas and Florida have established those financial mechanisms for participating agencies. Governors DeSantis (FL) and Abbott (TX), along with each state’s Attorney General and Republican legislators, have worked to effectively mandate participation for many law enforcement agencies, with an emphasis on agencies that operate a jail.

The Texas Immigration Law Council has this cool visual tracker of the active 287(g) agreements in the state.

In other words: the direct financial incentives aren’t necessarily the primary driver for a police superintendent or sheriff to sign up in these states, at least when compared to the prospect of losing out on state funding or access to other existing resources.

Bills similar to the Texas and Florida legislation have been introduced over the past three or four months in South Carolina, Tennessee, Kentucky, and several other Republican-led states. A similar effort in Idaho seems to have stalled out this week, after multiple attempts to force the legislation through the state senate that included a support letter from WH Deputy Assistant to the President Alex Meyer.

This post has already grown way too long, so I’ll have to save a longer breakdown of state-level policy for a future post. But the message from Republican legislators to law enforcement leaders is clear: partner with ICE, or else.

Community Pushback

There isn’t a single factor behind the increasing number of canceled agreements either. 45 agreements have been removed from ICE’s list since December 8th, in response to a county board vote, by executive order from Gov. Spanberger in Virginia, through state legislation in Maryland, in response to media inquiries and grassroots advocacy, and even legal challenges.

Last week, the Charlottesville City School Board passed a resolution to affirm the school district’s stance against cooperating with ICE, or any law enforcement agencies acting on its behalf under the 287(g) program. After the Caney Valley Public Schools drama, this quote from Virginia’s NPR affiliate (VPM) on Thursday stood out:

School board members Shymora Cooper and Zyahna Bryant connected the resolution with a campaign to re-vote on the return of school resource officers from the Charlottesville Police Department in city schools.

‘As I was reading over the resolution, I started to think about the conversation in reference to SROs, and if we’re going to protect students from ICE, I also want to protect our Black and brown communities from SROs,’ Cooper said.

Tracking canceled agreements

ICE does not publish a list of canceled agreements. Instead, they simply remove any canceled entries from the next published spreadsheet, in a way that makes it very difficult to track these changes (and when they happened). Which is why I’ve added a new tab section to my report, to help monitor these canceled agreements.

Interestingly, 11 of the 45 agreements removed since December 8th (when I started tracking these canceled agreements) were for agencies in Pennsylvania.
