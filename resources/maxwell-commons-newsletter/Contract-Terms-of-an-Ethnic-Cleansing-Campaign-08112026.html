Over the past week, we’ve learned several important details related to the federal government’s plans for thousands of immigrant children under its care. This includes two no-bid contracts, worth nearly $400 million, awarded to a boutique corporate law firm and an “anti-sex trafficking” nonprofit — to run a legal services program for these minors.

This comes in the immediate wake of the program’s previous contract expiring at the end of July, after a long legal battle and targeted harassment from Trump officials. Its former administrator, the Acacia Center for Justice, oversaw a network of over 100 subcontractor organizations supporting the program's services.

Procurement Language

UAC + HHS + ORR

The children in question entered the U.S. under an “unaccompanied alien children” (UAC) designation, and either don’t have an eligible custody sponsor available, are waiting on a pending custody transfer or other procedural step, or are otherwise unable or ineligible to exit ORR custody.

Notably, the Office of Refugee Resettlement is part of the Administration for Children & Families (ACF), inside RFK Jr.’s U.S. Department of Health & Human Services (HHS). That makes ORR unique, as one of the few immigration-related federal agencies that falls outside the core umbrella of the Departments of Homeland Security, State, and Justice.

Keep that distinction in mind, because some of the most jarring developments in this story are conveniently obscured behind an unofficial system of program abbreviations, procurement codes, and legal jargon. As you can already see above, I’ve emphasized the key abbreviations with bold text throughout this article to help track the complex coding system involved in this mess.

EOIR

First of all, I’ll just state explicitly that this lapse in representation was completely avoidable. And it created unnecessary, dangerous risk that children will appear for upcoming EOIR (immigration court) proceedings without a qualified attorney by their side.

As the primary service provider, Acacia was responsible for coordinating a network of more than 100 subcontractor nonprofits, including KIND, the Amica Center for Immigrant Rights, Ayuda, and Church World Service, to represent over 20,000 children in court.

Since January 2025, the Trump administration has hacked away at this network1:

  • In February 2025, HHS issued (and quickly rescinded) a stop-work order to the program’s sub-contracted nonprofit organizations, temporarily freezing funding for legal orientation, consultation, and attorney-of-record representation services.

  • The admin proceeded to try ending Acacia’s contract outright in March 2025, before turning to withheld payments to contracted organizations for services already completed.

  • In June 2026, DHS agents showed up at the D.C. offices of Amica, Ayuda, and KIND asking for records about the children they represent — sensitive personal and legal data that DHS has no right to.

  • On July 31, the Acacia contract officially expired after a series of temporary extensions ended. In an official statement, Acacia clarified that additional stop-gap funding extensions would have required the organization and its partners to hand over confidential client information to DHS.

One piece of good news in this cruel series of events: last week, a federal judge in the Northern District of California ordered the government to pay the roughly $65 million in unpaid services under this program. According to El Paso Matters, HHS officials confirmed on Friday, August 7th that they had complied with the court order and released the overdue payments to those orgs.

Burke Law Group: Up to $150 million

After initial reports earlier last week, a ‘notice of intent to award a single-source cooperative agreement’ was published to the Federal Register on August 6th, confirming ORR’s plan to pay up to $150 million to Burke Law Group, a boutique corporate firm in Houston.

According to the notice, Burke Law Group will take on legal orientation, consultation, and attorney-of-record representation services in immigration court (EOIR) proceedings + for cases managed by U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Services (USCIS) for thousands of children in ORR care under the UAC program. The one-year performance period runs from August 15, 2026 to August 14, 2027, and the scope of services under the proposed award also includes “limited discharge-related legal continuity planning and referral support for children exiting ORR care.”

From the Federal Register notice (bolded text added for emphasis):

“ORR announces the intent to award a single-source cooperative agreement to Burke Law Group to provide legal orientation, legal consultation, and attorney-of-record representation services, as required under 8 U.S.C. 1232(c)(5), 45 CFR 410.1309(a), and the preliminary injunction in Community Legal Services In East Palo Alto, et. al. v. HHS, et. al., No. 4:25-cv-02847 (N.D. Cal.), for eligible UACs during immigration proceedings before EOIR and in hearings and proceedings before USCIS while children remain in ORR care. The proposed award also includes limited discharge-related legal continuity planning and referral support.

The proposed award would expand ORR’s capacity to provide legal orientation, legal consultation, and attorney-of-record representation services for eligible UACs while complementing existing ORR-funded legal services.”

Now, I’m not an attorney, but I’ll just note what the government is doing here: using the same court injunction it spent the past year fighting as (part of) the reason it is now handing up to $150 million in ‘continuity’ contract services to a private law firm of its choosing, sans competitive bidding process.

It’s also important to note that Burke Law Group is, objectively, unequipped for the work it just signed up for. The boutique corporate law firm was founded less than three years ago, and just two of its 26-attorney team appears to practice U.S. immigration law in any capacity — and both appear to be new hires. One attorney’s LinkedIn profile shows an August 2026 start date at Burke, while the other’s doesn’t even reflect the job change yet.

And the second attorney’s LinkedIn profile and still-published law firm website show he ran a small niche practice, focused on H-2A/H-2B seasonal agricultural and hospitality visas. It’s definitely not an area of immigration law where you pick up loads of experience that you can then apply to helping run a massive legal services program representing thousands of traumatized and isolated kids in removal proceedings.

On August 7th, Rep. Joaquin Castro called for an independent investigation into how Burke was selected for this massive no-bid contract.

Our Rescue: $244 million

A second notice, posted to the federal procurement system, SAM.gov (or System for Award Management) on Friday, shows ORR awarded a separate $244 million to ‘Our Rescue’ — for what looks like the same, or similar, UAC program legal services as the $150 million awarded to Burke Law Group the day before.

The August 7th notice states:

“This contract was not awarded competitively and was not awarded on a fixed-price basis. The contract was awarded to ensure continuity of legal services for unaccompanied alien children.”

And while Our Rescue may not ring a bell, the organization’s old name might: Operation Underground Railroad (or ‘O.U.R.’). It’s an “anti-sex-trafficking” nonprofit founded by Tim Ballard, who resigned in 2023 in response to sexual assault allegations from multiple women, including former employees. And its current CEO, Derek Benner, spent over a decade at DHS, most recently as an executive associate director of Homeland Security Investigations (“HSI” is a component subagency of ICE), as Whitney Curry Wimbish notes in her new article in The American Prospect today, on Our Rescue’s ORR contract.

“Our Rescue conducts international sting operations to catch sex traffickers, similar to Dateline’s “To Catch a Predator.” The organization gained national notoriety in 2023, when the film Sound of Freedom dramatized the life of founder and CEO Tim Ballard, portraying him as a Homeland Security Investigations agent sent to rescue child sex trafficking victims in Colombia.”

The organization has a long, well-documented history of unverifiable human trafficking “rescue” claims, and also offers programming for local and state law enforcement. And if you, like me, were not already familiar, here’s a quote from the Sound of Freedom Wikipedia page:

The film attracted considerable attention for its connections to the QAnon conspiracy theory, a conspiracy theory that states a worldwide Satanic, cannibal cabal is running a mass human trafficking operation.”

Also, there’s no evidence the organization has ever provided immigration legal services to anyone, literally ever.

NOFO

Also, it’s not just the legal service contracts. In early June, ORR posted a “notice of funding opportunity“ (NOFO) on Grants.gov for a separate set of UAC-related services — in this case, “home study and post-release services“ for children released from ORR custody. On Monday, August 3rd, that NOFO was updated to:

  • more than double the total program funding, from $258 million to $555 million;

  • increase the number of expected awards, from 15 to 20 grants;

  • raise the ‘award ceiling’ for the first budget period, from $50 to $100 million.2

In other words: while the federal government was busy defunding legal services for these children, it was also pouring money into other program components —like post-release “monitoring”, which involves collecting more detailed data, including DNA, from children, their sponsors, and other household members.

We also learned more about the ‘National Coordination Center’ (NCC) that DHS is building in Tennessee to support the 1,800+ law enforcement agencies partnering with ICE through its massive 287(g) program.

Beyond coordinating detention transfers and pickups, earlier procurement records had indicated that ICE plans to use NCC resources to also support its UAC “safety verification initiative”. These plans were confirmed through the new contract documents with Koniag Government Services3, as Tennessee Lookout reports today (emphasis added)4:

The city of Adams, a Robertson County town of about 600 people near the Kentucky border, is identified in the database as the future site of the planned call center, intended to function round-the-clock to support local police and sheriffs’ departments across the country that have entered into cooperative agreements with Immigration and Customs Enforcement.

The call center will also help locate unaccompanied migrant children and develop new technologies for ICE, contract documents show.

The one-year contract, effective Aug. 6, was awarded to Koniag Government Services. The company is a subsidiary of Koniag, a for-profit Alaska Native corporation created for the benefit of 3,400 Alutiiq shareholders, according to its website.
