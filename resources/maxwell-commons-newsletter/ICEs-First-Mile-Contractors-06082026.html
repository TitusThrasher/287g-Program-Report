The First Mile Problem

In logistics, the ‘first mile’ represents the initial stages of a delivery route or transportation chain. Think of a local corner store where you can drop off packages for UPS or FedEx delivery. Or a fleet of trucks moving produce from local farms to a processing facility, long before it ends up on grocery store shelves.

Now consider what this first mile might look like across a massive immigration enforcement and deportation system. After all, it was former (as of last week) ICE acting director Todd Lyons who suggested treating the deportation process as a business.

You might remember how Lyons described his vision for the ICE deportation network last April: “like (Amazon) Prime, but with human beings.” His disastrous 17-month term as director ended last week, with former GEO Group executive David Venturella taking over the agency on June 1st.2

Venturella inherits the agency in the middle of a large-scale recruitment campaign and reorganization. And the agency’s massive portfolio of intergovernmental contracts with local police, county jails, and sheriffs’ offices. He’s also inherited the massive war chest of funding from last year’s OBBBA — with another $30+ billion likely on the way3. Business is good!

Misconduct is rampant, and there is growing evidence of large scale human rights abuses across the detention network. Racial profiling, blatant disregard for due process protections, dangerous detention conditions, violent enforcement tactics. The list goes on.

For this deadly machine, the first mile includes:

  • the initial encounters and arrests made by ICE and its partner agencies in local communities, at government offices and courthouses, etc.

  • apprehensions at, or near, the U.S. border by Customs and Border Protection (CBP);

  • detainer requests and custody transfers at county jails and state prisons.

Think of it as a funnel, where the larger top end represents the intake into the detention and deportation system — primarily through arrests and jail detainer requests. At the smaller bottom of the funnel, we have the ultimate goal of this machine as it is currently configured by Stephen Miller and Tom Homan: remove as many individuals as possible.

The detention network has incentives of its own, but should ultimately be seen as a component part of the larger deportation machine. The detention system enables the mass deportation agenda. Once someone is in ICE custody, the likelihood of their removal increases, including those who opt for “voluntary departure”, often coerced and encouraged by deplorable detention conditions.

“287(g)-Related” Arrests

A common question I get in response to my visual report on the 287(g) program: “Has the increase in active partnerships impacted ICE’s broader enforcement numbers?”

For reference, the program roster grew from 132 law enforcement agencies in early January 2025 to over 1,600 participating agencies today, in early June 2026. As I highlighted on Bluesky last week, we can reasonably expect over 2,200 agencies to have joined by the end of the calendar year.

So, has the 287(g) program’s massive growth had an impact on arrests?

The short answer: yes.

The slightly longer answer: The raw data from ICE is far from perfect but we can reasonably conclude the increase in active partnerships has already led to a shift in the composition of ICE arrests. The portion of ICE’s monthly arrests tagged as ‘287(g)-related’ (see below for more context) jumped from 2.9% in January 2025 to 9.5% in January 2026.4

The Data

The government data used in this new analysis, now available on my 287(g) program report, was provided by ICE in response to a FOIA request and processed by the Deportation Data Project. It covers 713,464 ‘Administrative arrests’ recorded by ICE between October 1, 2022 and March 10, 2026. This includes a unique record for “every time ICE arrests someone, whether or not that arrest results in a decision to detain the person.”

And for the purposes of my analysis and visual report on the 287(g) program, “287(g)-related arrests” includes 29,500 of those records tagged with at least one label that indicates the arrest was connected with the program:

  • ‘Apprehension method’ = “287(g) program”

  • ‘Final program’ = “287G Program” OR “287g Task Force”

  • ‘Operation’ = “HQ Tracking of Processing done by 287(g) authorized State & Local LEAs”

I mainly mention the variance across these three fields to illustrate how the data is coded by ICE, and how that impacts our (the public’s) ability to properly analyze it. As I’ve previously noted, even straightforward statistics become difficult to track when you’re working with DHS data.5

For my report’s purposes, there is no difference in weight or relative value across the three fields — if an arrest has one or more of these 287(g) tags, it’s included. It’s also filtered to already account for the 192 287(g)-related arrests marked as “likely duplicates” in Deportation Data Project’s processed version.

Solving the First Mile Locally

So, the overall composition in ICE arrest has shifted dramatically over the past year, with 287(g) related arrests representing nearly 10% of arrests in January and February 2026, up from less than 3% in early 2025. And the correlation between this shift and the relative growth in the agency’s total number arrests6 over the same period of time is statistically significant (r=0.86).

In other words, the growth in ICE’s 287(g) program has had a notable impact on the agency’s overall enforcement numbers. The first mile of our deadly deportation system is increasingly the territory of ICE’s local law enforcement partners.
