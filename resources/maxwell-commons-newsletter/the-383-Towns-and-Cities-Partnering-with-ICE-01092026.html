How We Got Here

Of the roughly 1,200 new agreements signed under ICE’s 287(g) program since January 2025, 381 are with municipal-level law enforcement agencies.

And while far more county-level agencies participate as of today, the number of towns and cities that have recently joined represents a new, dangerous layer to this ICE partnership program.

Most importantly: there were only two local-level agreements last January, both of which had carried over from the first Trump administration (signed in 2020).

Need to get up-to-speed on the 287(g) program? This post from December goes into more detail ⤵️

Also check out the Immigrant Legal Resource Center’s 287(g) explainer, FAQs, and other community resources. And here on Substack, often writes about the program in the context of other ICE enforcement and detention data.

Quick Recap:

The DHS 287(g) program allows local, county, and state law enforcement agencies to partner with ICE on federal immigration law enforcement activities.

There are three models of the program, with differences in how the ‘deputized’ officers are allowed to cooperate:

  • Jail Enforcement Model: A correctional officer, sheriff’s deputy, or other jail-based officer screens individuals already detained in their facility for immigration violations via ICE database.

  • Warrant Service Officer: Participating officers are trained and certified by ICE, and authorized to execute existing ICE administrative warrants in their jurisdiction.

  • Task Force Model: Participating agencies/officers are allowed to question individuals about immigration status, and arrest individuals for alleged immigration law violations in the course of their regular police work.

1 in 5 U.S. Counties

A majority of active agreements are with county-level agencies — primarily sheriffs’ offices. Nearly one in five U.S. counties1 participate as of today.

That makes sense when you consider that for most of the U.S., these offices are responsible for:

  • local jailing infrastructure;

  • warrant and summons services;

  • court security and bailiff duties; and/or

  • general patrolling and law enforcement duties in unincorporated areas, on county highways, etc.

In total, the agreements represent a dramatic shift in how local police are partnering with ICE for millions of people who live and work in these counties.

Local Police 🤝 ICE

While a smaller portion of the overall program expansion, the growth in participating municipalities is still staggering. For context, only two municipal-level agreements were active on January 20th, 2025.

Less than a year later, 383 towns and cities have agreements in place, representing a combined population of more than 9 million people. And while county agreements are crucial for the logistics network behind ICE’s deportation army, these municipal agreements are significant for their intimate impact on local policing.

In particular, virtually all of the local agreements are for the most invasive version of the 287(g) program — 97.5% are for the task force model in which police can question and make arrests for civil immigration violations.

For the millions of people who live, work, or otherwise travel through or spend time in these towns and cities, any interaction with local police can now theoretically escalate due to this program partnership.

Equally disturbing: some of these municipal-level agreements aren’t really municipalities.

ICE now has an active task force agreement with campus police for 15 colleges and universities in Florida. And because of a mandate from Governor DeSantis to get every law enforcement agency in the state onboard, there are even three Florida airports (Melbourne, Sarasota, and Sanford) with active agreements.

In last week’s review of 2025, I also discussed the 14 state-level agencies in Florida participating, including the state’s fish and wildlife services, lottery services, and its gaming control commission.

With ICE’s continued efforts to get data from federal agencies like the IRS, HHS etc. it’s also important to consider the sheer volume of data managed by these municipal, county, and state governments — and what that could mean *now or in the future) by officially partnering with ICE.

What Else

Here’s what else stands out to me in the municipal-level data:

  • Pennsylvania has the 2nd most towns/cities participating. Far behind Florida’s whopping 186 municipal agreements, but PA has 38 active municipalities covering a population of at least 200,000 — notably, most of these are for borough and township constable’s offices.

  • Following Pennsylvania in 3rd is Texas, where 33 municipalities have signed on. However, most of these are for small rural jurisdictions: the largest is League City (pop. 118,456), while six have less than 1,000 residents.

  • Relative to their county-level participation, very few towns/cities in Georgia, Kentucky, North Carolina, and Virginia have signed on (yet).

What I’m Watching

As I noted earlier this week, I fully expect participation to continue its current growth in 2026.

With Texas Senate Bill 8 kicking in last week — virtually requiring every agency with a jail to participate by December 2026 — we’re sure to see more agreements there, and in other states where legislators and governors are considering formal mandates or legislation to compel agencies to sign up.

For local police, though, the incentives recently became even more obvious. As I wrote on Tuesday, ICE started reimbursing payroll (and overtime) for participating officers at partner agencies starting October 1st, 2025.

They’re also offering to cover equipment expenses (laptops, phones, etc.) up to $7,500 per officer + up to $100,000 as stipends for new vehicles for participating agencies. And most concerning: the new benefits also include a cash incentive program, with quota-based payouts depending on the number of people handed to ICE. Seriously.

In the wake of Renee Good’s murder on Wednesday, all while shedding light on ICE abuses of power in her neighborhood, we need to pay attention to this program and what it means for the mobilization of ICE’s deportation army.
