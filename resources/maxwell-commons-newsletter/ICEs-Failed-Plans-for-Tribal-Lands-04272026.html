About four months after the first references to a ‘Tribal Task Force’ version of the 287(g) agreement were added to the agency’s landing page, ICE seems to have given up on recruiting a single tribal law enforcement agency to the program. All mention of the mysterious fourth version of its massive partnership scheme was quietly removed from ICE’s website on Friday.

I’ll be requesting more information from ICE and Homeland Security to confirm the program’s status. In the meantime, I wanted to write a bit about why this is significant, and how it connects to some other stories worth following.

Tribal Sovereignty in the Face of ICE

What led to ICE soft-launching the Tribal Task Force model in the first place, especially if it hadn’t already lined up initial participants? It’s hard to say.

In late December, I wrote about one of the early signs that ICE was moving forward with its ‘warehouse-to-concentration camp’ conversion plans — a no-bid contract to Kpb Services LLC for due diligence and “concept design” services related to retrofitting the massive warehouses into prison camps.

Notably, KPB was a joint venture business that included the Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation, through its Prairie Band LLC. After community members learned of the ICE/DHS contract in early December, Prairie Band disinvested from the joint venture and fired several executives involved in securing the contract.

“Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation and Prairie Band LLC understand the concerns raised about a recently awarded Department of Homeland Security planning contract that involves feasibility studies and technical assessments,” the statement said. “As a sovereign Nation, our values guide the decisions we make, and we acknowledge that this contract does not align with those principles.”
– From a statement from Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation, as quoted by Levi Rickert (Native News Online, December 10, 2025)

The Prairie Band Tribal Council also published a “Resolution Affirming Tribal Jurisdiction in Relation to Federal Immigration Enforcement (ICE) and U.S. Customs and Border Protection” in early February. The Resolution reaffirms the Potawatomi Nation’s sovereignty and asserts “jurisdiction, consent, and individual rights on the Nation’s trust and Tribal lands.”

Interestingly, the Resolution also provides clear direction to the Nation’s Tribal Police Department when interacting with ICE or CBP officers:

“The Tribal Council directs the officers of the Tribal Police Department during any interaction with federal immigration activity within the Nation’s Reservation to document the interaction; activate body-worn cameras during the duration of the contact, capturing any use of force, detentions, injuries, or other enforcement activities; ensure the safety of civilians and fellow Tribal Police Department officers; for any injured person on the scene of civil immigration enforcement immediately summon emergency medical services and make all reasonable efforts to render aid; if an officer receives information from the public or observes conduct by federal agents that constitutes apparent violations of Tribal or Federal law they shall enforce the applicable law, which may include issuing citations or taking the offender into custody; and that at no time shall the actions of a Tribal Police officer threaten the safety of civilians or fellow Tribal Police Officers.”

And in February, I wrote about the Oglala Sioux, whose Tribal Council voted unanimously against participating in the 287(g) program and to ban ICE from operating in the Nation’s Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota.

The impact of immigration enforcement on the Oglala Sioux Nation also came up in the context of the federal government’s violent campaign through Minnesota during Operation Metro Surge. It was feared that several Sioux Nation members in the Twin Cities may have been swept up during one of many indiscriminate raids where ICE and CBP targeted unhoused neighbors.

And when we covered ICE’s very brief partnership with a small K-12 school police force in March, one of the concerns and I highlighted was the relatively large demographic of students in the district who identify as Native American. Notably, the Caney Valley Public School district serves two small towns just outside the Osage Nation Reservation in northwest Oklahoma.

Rewriting History

This is all to say that a weaponized Department of Homeland Security represents the latest in a long history of attacks on the sovereignty of tribal nations by our federal government.

We were reminded of this during Metro Surge, when the Whipple Federal Building was turned into a base of operations for the thousands of feds operating in Minnesota from ICE, CBP, FBI, and other federal law enforcement agencies. The massive building currently houses Minnesota’s only immigration court, as well as office space for ICE and many other federal agencies with a presence in the Twin Cities. It also sits on land where the U.S. Army built Fort Snelling in 1805, as the headquarters for the federal government’s “monopoly of violence” against the Dakota people living in the area.

And if you followed the Supreme Court’s oral arguments on birthright citizenship (Trump v. Barbara) earlier this month, you may have noticed mention of tribal sovereignty came up within the context of the Solicitor General’s sloppy attempt to narrowly define who is protected by the Constitution and its 14th Amendment.

This explainer from the Native American Rights Fund is a really helpful overview of the SCOTUS argument and the messy history of tribal sovereignty and U.S. citizenship. 0 for 234

According to the Department of Justice’s justice statistics bureau, there are 258 tribal law enforcement agencies operating within the United States. 234 of those agencies are run by their respective peoples and tribal authorities, while 23 are operated by the Bureau of Indian Affairs (a component of the U.S. Department of the Interior).

If Friday’s updates to the 287(g) landing page are, in fact, confirmation that ICE will no longer be pursuing partnerships with tribal agencies, ICE went zero for 234 in recruiting independently-run tribal law enforcement agencies to the program. A Gap in the Denominator

That 0.00 batting average is particularly significant when you consider there are now 1,462 agencies participating in one (or more) of three existing program models. With roughly 18,000 total agencies currently in the United States, some conservative back-of-napkin math tells us the participation rate across all law enforcement agencies in the country is about 8.1%.

That rate jumps even higher when you consider hundreds of local police departments, county sheriffs’ offices, and state law enforcement agencies across ten states are barred from joining the program.3

Participation is now explicitly, or at least effectively, banned for agencies in ten states, including the largest by population, California (39.4 million), and the sixth largest, Illinois (pop. 12.7 million). All in, the ten states that restrict agencies from joining represent a population of nearly 88.5 million people.

Hawaii and Rhode Island, meanwhile, are unique as the only states where:

  1. there is no statewide restriction that prevents agencies from joining; AND

  2. there are zero active agencies participating in the program today.

While local resistance is certainly growing in states like Pennsylvania, where eleven 287(g) agreements have been canceled since December, ICE is adding too many new partnerships nationwide to keep pace. In fact, the program is growing faster today than it was three months ago.

And yet, ICE came up with a giant goose egg when trying to recruit tribal law enforcement agencies. That, I think, is an important story worth sharing.

P.S. The Wayback Machine

As I mentioned to in February, I’ve frequently turned to the Wayback Machine to track changes to the 287(g) program website and monitor other aspects of the program’s growth.

I also highlighted how it helped narrow down to a 4-hour period when someone at ICE quietly removed all references to its ‘Pending Agreements List’ from its website in early March. Previously, ICE published a second spreadsheet on its 287(g) landing page with basic details about each agency that had submitted an agreement but were still waiting for an ICE official to review and counter-sign.

(…) one tool I’ve used to help make sense of this massive program growth has been the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine, which lets you view previous versions of a webpage captured at specific date/timestamps.

In this case, you can confirm down to a four hour period when someone with access to ICE’s website removed the pending list: yesterday afternoon between 12:38pm and 4:28pm CST — shown as the last two snapshots on March 2nd in this screenshot of the Wayback Machine calendar view (in GMT).

And for last week’s edits to remove mention of the Tribal Task Force model, Wayback Machine’s snapshots let me narrow down the changes to a 17-hour period between Thursday at 12:01pm and Friday morning at 5:05am CDT.

Support Public Resources

If you’re not already familiar, the Wayback Machine is a key tool for journalists and researchers (and others) who need access to the digital archives behind the version of the internet you and I see in our browsers today.

Unfortunately, the Wayback Machine appears to be endangered, largely due to a shift in website access policies from major media orgs, like the New York Times and USAToday, to internet forums including Reddit. While these policies are largely a response to new AI scraping tools, there’s hope for a solution that would enable the Wayback Machine to access these sites without requiring publishers to put writers’ work at risk.

You can read more about the dilemma and what’s at stake here (from the Electronic Frontier Foundation), here (from Wired), and here (from Nieman Lab).
