The Cow

If you’re a local police chief looking for extra cash for your department, Homeland Security’s 287(g) program has quickly become a fantastic source of supplementary funding. And in some cases already, this new funding seems to go far beyond supplementary — bordering on transformative for a small municipal police department or sheriff’s office.

An article from the San Antonio Report yesterday covering the multiple active 287(g) agreements in Bexar County, TX (where San Antonio is the county seat1) revealed just how much money, exactly, we’re talking:

“‘Hundreds of precincts across Texas have already been doing so. I’ve gotten calls from a couple of them, where they train their officers and have very minimal participation. Then they had a case where they had a referral, and the federal government wrote them a check for nearly $300,000.’”

And while that might sound like exaggeration, consider:

  1. the quote was from Bexar County’s Precinct 3 Constable, Mark Vojvodich;

  2. in October, DHS officially started a new ‘reimbursement’ system for 287(g) participating agencies.

From the official DHS announcement on the new reimbursement system, from September:

“Starting October 1, 2025, participating law enforcement will have these reimbursement opportunities:

  • ICE will fully reimburse participating agencies for the annual salary and benefits of each eligible trained 287(g) officer, including overtime coverage up to 25% of the officer’s annual salary.

  • Law enforcement agencies will be eligible for quarterly monetary performance awards based on the successful location of illegal aliens provided by ICE and overall assistance to further ICE’s mission to Defend the Homeland:

  • 90% - 100% - $1,000 per eligible task force officer

  • 80% - 89% - $750 per eligible task force officer

  • 70% - 79% - $500 per eligible task force officer”

If it sounds like our government is running a quota-based rewards program encouraging local police to round up more people for ICE, it’s because that’s exactly what is happening.

They don’t even try to hide it — the announcement includes this long-winded, unashamedly political statement from ICE Deputy Director Madison Sheahan:

“ICE is not only supercharging our hiring, we are also multiplying partnerships with state and local law enforcement to remove the worst of the worst including murderers, gang members, rapists, terrorists, and pedophiles from our country. Thanks to the One Big Beautiful Bill, ICE launched a new reimbursement program for state and local law enforcement who partner with DHS to make America safe again,” said ICE Deputy Director Madison Sheahan.

“We encourage all state and local law enforcement agencies to sign a 287(g) agreement now. By joining forces with ICE, you’re not just gaining access to these unprecedented reimbursement opportunities—you’re becoming part of a national effort to ensure the safety of every American family.”

Rewards over Risk

The Rewards

The DHS announcement wraps up with this blunt message on the program’s intent:

“DHS encourages all state and local law enforcement agencies to sign a 287(g) agreement to help defend the homeland and to gain access to these reimbursement opportunities.”

This messaging is important to note. The program’s expenses — and the potential legal (and financial) liability taken on by participating agencies — are common arguments against joining. With this new reimbursement and rewards system designed to incentivize new agencies, those arguments will likely weaken as jurisdictions opt for the easy revenue and accept the increased risk in liability.

I suspect this, in turn, will impact how states respond to the program’s rapid growth. In Florida, this means an ‘all-in’ sprint to compel every law enforcement agency in the state to sign up. And the program cash is already paying out for some of the state’s participating agencies. And as I noted last week, Texas’ SB 8 took effect January 1st, requiring every law enforcement agency in the state with a jail to have an active 287(g) agreement by December 2026.

A fantastic breakdown from Tennessee Lookout in November includes this great explanation of the new financial benefits, and how it impacts program advertising in the state:

ICE agreements in the past have come with no financial compensation beyond training provided by federal authorities and IT equipment allowing communication with federal immigration databases.

But the Trump administration included $14 billion in federal funding in the Big Beautiful Bill Act in July to fund financial incentives for local and state law enforcement agencies who agreed to ink 287(g) deals by Oct. 1.

They include full reimbursement for the annual salary, benefits and overtime for each 287(g) trained officer, $7,500 for laptops and cell phones per trained officer and up to $100,000 toward the purchase of agency vehicles.

The Risk

I expect other states will look for ways to limit (or further limit) participation.

That might include state legislation, similar to what Illinois, California, Washington, and seeral other states already have in place. After a previous effort failed last year, Maryland legislators (with new support) plan to push again for a statewide ban on the agreements in 2026.

In Minnesota, Attorney General Keith Ellison published an opinion in December clarifying that county sheriffs in the state can’t enter into a 287(g) agreement with ICE. Instead, Ellison’s opinion places that authority in the hands of each county’s board of commissioners, thereby forcing the agreements to presumably go through actual legal review before signing.

This was the path New Jersey took in ending statewide 287(g) participation back in 2019, when NJ Attorney General Gurbir Grewal amended a state directive to practically abolish the program in the state.

And while some might argue a unilateral directive or legal opinion from a state’s attorney general is not the same as legislation, it makes sense why an AG might see the program as a giant legal risk for their state that requires urgent intervention:

The litigation costs can be enormous. Maricopa County, Arizona, was ordered to pay $43 million in litigation fees due to lawsuits directly related to its 287(g) program, largely attributed to racial profiling and discrimination against immigrant communities.

Other localities have also seen significant legal costs. In 2017, Los Angeles County [paid $255,000] to settle one named plaintiff’s detainer claim. The same year, San Francisco paid a $190,000 settlement to an individual unlawfully turned over to ICE, and Spokane settled a detainer lawsuit for $49,000. In 2018, San Juan County paid $300,000 to settle a detainer class-action lawsuit, and paid named plaintiffs additional sums to settle their claims.”
– “287(g) Agreements: A Costly Choice for Localities” (Niskanen Center, October 2020)

Litigation volume will almost definitely skyrocket as hundreds of new agencies get fully onboarded over the coming months. That’s particularly true with ICE’s new payroll reimbursement and incentive system kicking in.

Replacing the agency’s once-colorful messaging: a monotonous mouthpiece of racist dog whistles, lots of “security” talk, and a bunch of cringe posts celebrating ICE arrests.
