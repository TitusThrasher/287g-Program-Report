# A Reporting Field Guide to 287(g)

## What it covers:

This is a practical guide for local reporters, investigative journalists, and independent media seeking public records related to ICE’s 287(g) program and its implementation at participating agencies. It assumes you already know the basics of how the 287(g) program works — to learn more before diving into this guide, [see here](/resources/README.md) (and stay tuned for more resources).

This includes:

* a **program overview** and general explainer (see '[Program Overview](/resources/reporting-field-guide/Program-Overview.md)');

* a breakdown of ICE's layered **contract chain**, and what each layer reveals (see ‘[Document Chain](/resources/reporting-field-guide/Document-Chain.md)’);

* a summary of the program's various funding sources and **financial incentives** (see '[The Money](/resources/reporting-field-guide/The-Money.md)');
  
* highlights of the various **authorization-related challenges** tied to the program's hasty growth and premature agency signups (see '[Authorization Process](/resources/reporting-field-guide/Authorization-Process.md)');

* understanding the specific **legal language** that ICE uses across its contract system (see ‘[ICE Contract Language](/resources/reporting-field-guide/ICE-Contract-Language.md)’);

* how to send **federal FOIA** requests to ICE, and what you can realistically expect in response (see ‘[Federal FOIA]()’);

* what to include for **county and municipal records requests** (see ‘[State + Local Records]()’) and the documents that have been produced from similar requests (see ‘[Resources + References]()’).

It does ***not*** include fill-in-the-blank template letters, primarily because of the wide variation in format, length + content requirements, etc. across government agencies. Instead, I’ve focused this guide on what to ask for, where to look, and how to frame your requests.

## TL;DR Brief

Do *not* start with ICE. Start with the participating agency and, when applicable, the municipal or county government that authorized the agreement (see ‘[Authorization Process](/resources/reporting-field-guide/Authorization-Process.md)’). The sheriff's office or police department, the town/county auditor, or the town council/county board hold the same (or similar) contract documents and reimbursement requests \+ payment records.

ICE's own agreement instructs partner agencies to clear disclosures with ICE first — but it also contains a carve-out preserving state public records compliance, and it expressly authorizes agencies to give the signed agreement to the media upon request.

> “ICE has been telling local governments that partner with the agency what they can and can’t release to the public, even under their own state laws. The Dallas Morning News [reported](https://www.dallasnews.com/news/politics/article/ice-texas-public-records-states-rights-secrecy-22291873.php) that in April, ICE emailed jurisdictions with 287(g) agreements, asserting that the “prohibition on \[an\] agency’s unilateral release of ICE information is expansive” and ordered them to “immediately consult with the ICE FOIA office, ICE Public Affairs, or your local Field Office Director” before responding to any public records requests."
>
> “Even if the documents were available from ICE, the federal FOIA is not an adequate alternative. ICE often ignores FOIA requests, all too often releasing records only after requesters sue the agency in federal court. Not all requesters have the resources to do so, and litigation often takes months."
>
> '[ICE Is Trying to Hide State Records of People in Detention. We Filed a FOIA to Find Out Why](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/blog/ice-hide-state-records-people-in-detention-foia/)' by **Meghan Conley** (Tennessee Immigrant and Refugee Rights Coalition), and **Raul Pinto** (American Immigration Council)
