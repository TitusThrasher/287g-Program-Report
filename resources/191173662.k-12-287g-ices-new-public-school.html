When Exposed to Sunshine, Ice Will Melt

Today, March 16th, is James Madison’s birthday. Despite snowy weather here in Chicago, happy Sunshine Week to those who partake!

As the author of the First Amendment, Madison’s birthday marks National Freedom of Information Day and anchors Sunshine Week — a collaborative effort from journalists, educators, government officials and civic leaders, and private business partners to advocate for government transparency through public records.1

With that in mind, today’s post is focused on some important updates re: our 287(g) agreement report and public accountability.

Not familiar with the 287(g) program, or want to learn more before reading further? My program overview page explains how it works, and why its rapid growth deserves your attention:

ICE’s Newest Police Partners

The Excel list published on ICE’s 287(g) program page today includes 11 new agreements, pushing the program total to 1,528. There were just 134 agreements active on January 20th, 2025.

Two of the new agreements stand out:

Caney Valley Public Schools

Caney Valley Public Schools, in Washington County, Oklahoma, is the first K-12 school system to sign up for the ICE partnership program. Yes, a public school district just voluntarily signed up to partner with ICE.

Worse yet, Caney Valley’s participation is through a Task Force Model agreement, meaning the school system’s resource officers will have wide authority to question residents and make arrests for suspected civil immigration violations.

At least 17 Florida colleges and universities with campus police departments have already signed on to the 287(g) program since January 2025. But we’ve yet to see how a task force ICE partnership might operate within a rural elementary/middle school or high school, like Caney Valley’s.

Personally, I hope to see this agreement added to our canceled list as soon as possible.

Missouri’s COMET Drug Task Force

The other agreement from today’s additions that caught my eye: the COMET (“Combined Ozarks Multi-Jurisdictional Enforcement Team”) Drug Task Force, based in Greene County, Missouri.

First, because I’m not even sure if the agreement is listed correctly by ICE. It’s listed as a municipal-level Task Force agreement in Greene County, Missouri — but other documentation for COMET indicates it’s primarily a county-level task force, with state and federal funding, that incorporates several sheriffs’ offices in the Ozarks region.

We’ll have to wait for ICE to add a link to the actual document to confirm, which is typically added within a week or two via a subsequent update of the official agency list.

In the meantime, let’s see what else we can learn about the COMET Drug Task Force and its operations in southwestern Missouri. Like this great breakdown of Missouri’s drug task forces and their “cowboy” tactics, from the Pulitzer Center (2020):

Some of the task forces operating in secret have abused their power, critics say, by using coercive tactics, buying expensive surveillance equipment and focusing extensively on marijuana instead of more dangerous drugs. (…)

The Comet drug task force in Missouri's Ozarks region withheld records based on a terrorism exception to Sunshine Law.”2
