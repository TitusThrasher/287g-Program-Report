For those who aren’t already familiar, Austin is a research assistant professor Syracuse University’s Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse (TRAC) who has been researching immigration enforcement policy and data for 15+ years — one of the OG experts in ICE data. He’s also the co-founder of Relevant Research, a studio helping other researchers manage and amplify their work online.

Austin’s work is routinely cited by a wide range of journalists and media, used by immigration law professionals in casework, and read by thousands of followers across his Substack newsletter + social media (Bluesky, LinkedIn, Instagram).

Having worked in immigration law for nearly a decade, I can confidently say Austin’s work is everywhere — and for good reason. Along with his research on ICE detention data and the EOIR courts (and their massive case backlog), he cut his teeth studying 287(g) agreements, and his insights have been incredibly helpful while developing my report on the program.

Needless to say, I was very excited when he asked me to jump on Substack Live to chat about the 287(g) program, government transparency, and what it takes to make this data ‘usable’. No more pending agreement list?

And it turns out our conversation was a great primer for the 72 (!!) new agreements ICE added to its list of active agreements just a few hours later.

Here’s where things get interesting: until today, ICE’s 287(g) landing page included two download links: one for the active agreements, and a second for pending agreements.

As of yesterday, the pending agreement list is no longer available.

As I mentioned to Austin, one tool I’ve used to help make sense of this massive program growth has been the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine, which lets you view previous versions of a webpage captured at specific date/timestamps.

In this case, you can confirm down to a four hour period when someone with access to ICE’s website removed the pending list: yesterday afternoon between 12:38pm and 4:28pm CST — shown as the last two snapshots on March 2nd in this screenshot of the Wayback Machine calendar view (in GMT).

As you can see from the screenshots below, the ‘Pending Agreements’ section was completely removed between the 18:39:29 and 22:28:36 snapshots (again, in GMT):

Why did ICE decide to stop publishing the pending agency list yesterday? I can only guess. But it’s important to note that this pending list had just 11 agreements listed on of Friday morning, shortly before the new active agreement list was published…with 70+ additions.

The math is not, in fact, mathing.

The Tenaha, Texas Police Department’s Task Force agreement was signed by ICE on January 7th, 2026 — but is listed as January 7th, 2025 on ICE’s list. That is, of course, two weeks before Trump took office and gave ICE the green light to start processing new 287(g) applications.

And an example of why government transparency should include a requirement for unique identifiers in published data sets like this. Here are two agreements (for the Task Force and Warrant Service program models) for the same sheriff’s office, signed by ICE on the same day.

This is not the output of a serious effort by ICE at providing transparent info and data on how this program is scaling. Particularly so when you consider Kristi Noem and DHS have added new bounty-style incentives and reimbursement perks for participating agencies — with a notably absent paper trail so far.

While far from a surprise based on the agency’s conduct across the board today, I think it’s important that we keep tabs on each of these moving parts. Which is why I’ll keep updating our report as long as this program continues to change shape.
