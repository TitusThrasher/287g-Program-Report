The Cost of Safety in Gratis, Ohio

In April, two local cops from a small town in rural Ohio drove 50 miles to Cincinnati, in an effort to perform “wellness checks” on up to 30 children identified by ICE.

The two officers from Gratis, Ohio were almost certainly not doing this work for free1. Hidden in a non-public service agreement with ICE’s Task Force 287(g) partner agencies, and a similarly hard-to-obtain contract rider, are more details on the motivations that drove these cops to Cincinnati. Specifically, a series of cash incentives — up to $22,500 per child — paid to the officer’s agency for completing “safety verifications”2 on minors who entered the country as ‘unaccompanied alien children’ (UAC) during the Biden administration.3

According to Markwayne Mullin, Tom Homan, Stephen Miller, and the rest of the ghoulish cheerleaders of our deadly deportation machine, up to 450,000 children and young adults are “missing” under this UAC designation. They are lying, but for what purpose I’m not sure.

“But Jennifer Podkul, chief of global policy and advocacy at KIND, a nonprofit organization that advocates for unaccompanied minors, said calling the children lost is a “fundamental misunderstanding” of the process and the report.” – PolitiFact (March 2026)

For more background information on how this ‘skip tracing’ work was incorporated into the scope of the Task Force 287(g) program, this November 2025 article from is a great overview.

Walks like a bounty, quacks like a bounty

A per-child bounty, written into an intergovernmental service agreement and an unsigned contract rider. Now consider ICE and its partner agencies have actively resisted public record requests for these documents.

How did these wellness checks get baked into the Task Force 287(g) program? The ICE landing page doesn’t mention anything about it. The downloadable Memorandum of Agreement template doesn’t either.

The scope of this work, and the money that comes with it, is hidden in a second and third layer of contract documents, including a service-level agreement and an incentives attachment.

Before we get into those documents, though: how did the 287(g) program quietly grow into a dangerous new mission for ICE and its local and state police partners to target hundreds of thousands of Black and Brown immigrant kids?

Money, of course.

Recap: The 287(g) Cash Cow

As of today, July 17th, 2026, there are 1,421 active Task Force Model 287(g) agreements, representing about 8% of the 17,500 to 18,000 law enforcement agencies in the United States.4

Learn more about the 287(g) program

Roughly 2.6 agencies have signed up every single day since the inauguration on January 20, 2025.

From the program’s establishment until October 2025, the only costs related to the program that were covered by DHS were training expenses and limited software reimbursements for participating agencies. This changed with last year’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), which effectively handed ICE’s Enforcement and Removal Operations a multi-billion dollar slush fund to expand the 287(g) program.

In September 2025, DHS began marketing a new set of financial incentives to agencies to encourage them to sign up for the Task Force Model 287(g), with ‘reimbursements’ supposedly effective starting October 1st. The incentives include:

  • Vehicles: effectively, a rubber-stamp grant for up to about $100,000 per agency.

  • Technology: subscription costs for ICE’s SaaS surveillance tools and data.

  • Payroll: salary, benefits, and overtime for participating officers.

  • Quota-based bonuses: cash incentives tied to arrests and other work assigned by ICE.

Missing receipts

When you consider the above, it’s particularly concerning that tracking the billions funneling through this program has been incredibly difficult.

We have a limited understanding of how reimbursement requests are actually processed, and we know even less about which agencies have actually been paid, when, and for how much. And like most of this program’s operations, what we do know is largely thanks to the persistence of local journalists who’ve won access through public records fights, and at least one leaked agency document.

For example, the Owyhee County Sheriff’s Office in rural Idaho. As Rachel Spacek at InvestigateWest revealed in late April:

“DHS paid $115,000 to the Owyhee County Sheriff’s Office on Jan. 30 for equipment related to 287(g). The office later spent that money on two vehicles — a Ford Explorer and Ford Interceptor, both 2025 models — and body cameras for two officers.”

It’s also a program with very few requirements — or even a proper vetting process, as we learned when a K-12 school resource officer in Oklahoma “mistakenly” joined the program earlier this year.

State grant kickbacks

The federal money is only part of it. Several states have also established their own grant programs to encourage more agencies to join.

In Tennessee, records obtained by WTVC show a $75 million state funding program for sheriffs’ offices, allegedly dedicated to ‘violent crime reduction’, that requires them to ‘demonstrate’ they have taken steps toward a 287(g) agreement.

The Texas legislature passed Senate Bill 8 last year, which requires most of the state’s county sheriffs to sign a 287(g) agreement or join “a similar federal program.” Counties that don’t comply by December 2026 may be sued by the Texas attorney general, but SB 8 also established a grant program to encourage sheriffs to join. Managed by the state comptroller, the program has awarded a total of $4.9 million to 59 counties since January 1st.

“According to documentation from the Auditor’s Office, the Fayette County Sheriff’s Office has received $725,500 from the federal government through the 287(g) program. So far, the Sheriff’s Office has spent $248,378.43 of those funds on equipment purchases.” - Andy Behlen, Fayette County Record (July 16, 2026)

Florida runs an even larger version: the State Board of Immigration Enforcement — which is made up of Governor Ron DeSantis’ cabinet officials — has confirmed over $21 million in grants to sheriffs and local police departments. The largest SBIE grant so far, totaling “over $9.8 million”, was awarded to the Lee County Sheriff’s Office in May. And with at least $250 million in state funding set aside by the SBIE, the confirmed awards represent a small fraction (less than 10%) of the earmarked total.

Slush fund refills

Oh, by the way: more money is on the way. The OBBBA also authorized a $3 billion DOJ grant program for 287(g) agencies, mockingly titled ‘Bridging Immigration-Related Deficits Experienced Nationwide’ (or “BIDEN”) by Republican legislators. Applications for the first round of program grants were due this past Wednesday, July 15th.

And in early June, Republicans voted to give even more money to ICE and CBP — with even less oversight than usual attached to it. The reconciliation bill includes very little substance, but does indicate that more 287(g) funding is on the way. As the American Immigration Council explained:

“ICE: $38.2 billion to expand and sustain enforcement operations by hiring and equipping personnel across its divisions, supporting detention and removal transportation, upgrading technology and facilities, and expanding 287(g) agreements with local law enforcement. $7.5 billion of this funding is specifically earmarked for ICE’s Homeland Security Investigations for ‘non-immigration’ purposes.”

UAC Safety Verification Initiative

Ok, so we know the task force model 287(g) ‘memorandum of agreement’ — the public document with boilerplate contract language — doesn’t mention UAC work specifically. How, then, do we even know what these ‘wellness checks’ are?

ICE has baked this UAC work, including the bounty incentives structure, into a second, harder-to-obtain service agreement signed by each agency. That is the contract layer where contract terms, including a brief reference to the UAC bounty-style financial incentives, are explained in more detail.

Owyhee County Sheriff’s Office

The service agreement signed by Owyhee County Sheriff Larry Kendrick in Idaho, obtained and published by Rachel Spacek and the InvestigateWest team in late April (‘Rural Idaho sheriff’s office got at least $116,000 to help ICE’), was the first explicit reference to UAC-related work in a 287(g) program contract.

According to ICE’s service agreement with the sheriff’s office, UAC work includes:

“any case specifically designated by ICE that was previously in Office of Refugee Resettlement custody, subsequently released, and unaccounted for.”

The agreement also outlines the reporting expectations for participating agencies:

“The service provider shall provide ERO with hours spent performing ICE enforcement activities for adult and Unaccompanied Alien Children (UAC), for each credentialed TFO [Task Force Officer] assigned to the apprehension.”

I’ll just note that while I’m not a lawyer, ‘apprehension’ and ‘wellness check’ strike me as very different things that aren’t typically used interchangeably.

Bradley County Constables

With the service-level agreement, we knew this UAC '“work” was now within the self-defined scope of the 287(g) partnership program. But it doesn’t explain how we know the exact bounty amount ICE is offering to pay these local agencies.

That’s where ongoing coverage from Jules Feeney at the Chattanooga Times Free Press comes in. Feeney’s investigation and reporting on several elected constables from Bradley County, TN highlighted they had traveled to at least three other states under this new ‘287(g) UAC Safety Verification Initiative’.

Thanks to that work, we now have access to the third layer of ICE’s contracting system, in the form of a “UAC Safety Verification Initiative Incentives Program for 287(g) Partners.”

This UAC-specific document is referenced as an unsigned attachment — effectively a contract rider — in the agency-specific service agreement (the second contract layer, see above), which briefly refers to this fabricated docket of 450,000 UAC “wellness checks”. This new third layer, however, lays out the expectations and financial incentives in far more detail. And those details are disturbing.

“Incentive Opportunities: Two incentive categories are available to agencies participating in the initiative. When combined, they amount to $22,500 in incentives per UAC case, depending on the scope of the work activities completed for each UAC case.

  1. "Case Incentives" - Up to $2,500 per case

    Case incentives recognize the 287(g) partner's completion of initial address verification and residence confirmation activities as described in the table below”

  1. "Safety Verification" Incentives - Up to $20,000 per case

    Safety verification incentives reward the 287(g) partner for more comprehensive safety and welfare verification activities which protect children and communities as described in the table below"

And that, folks, is how we get a per-child bounty explicitly written into a federal intergovernmental contract. Anyways, it’s time to tear it all down.
